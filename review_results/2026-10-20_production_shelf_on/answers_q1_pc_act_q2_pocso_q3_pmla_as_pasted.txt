Q1. Can a disproportionate assets case under the PC Act be filed without section 17 approval?

Ans - Whether a disproportionate assets case can be filed without the order Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act talks about depends on who is investigating and at what rank — not every filing needs it, but certain officers do.

What Section 17 requires

Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act says that below a certain rank, no police officer can investigate an offence under this Act, or arrest without a warrant for it, without the order of a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Magistrate of the first class. The ranks are: an Inspector for the Delhi Special Police Establishment, an Assistant Commissioner of Police in the named metropolitan areas, and a Deputy Superintendent of Police (or equivalent) elsewhere. This exists so that corruption investigations, which can touch a public servant's reputation and career, are not handled casually by junior officers. The section's first proviso lets a State Government authorise an Inspector-rank officer generally or specially to investigate without that Magistrate's order. The second proviso says an offence falling under clause (b) of sub-section (1) of Section 13 of the Act — which, per that section, covers a public servant intentionally enriching himself illicitly, including the "disproportionate assets" situation described in its Explanation 1 — cannot be investigated without an order from an officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police.

So for a disproportionate assets case, which falls under Section 13(1)(b), the real gatekeeper is not a magistrate's order for every investigation, but specifically a Superintendent of Police's order before investigation starts, in addition to the rank/magistrate rule for officers below the stated grades.

What to keep in mind

- Section 17 governs who can investigate and arrest without warrant; it does not require prior judicial approval before an FIR is even registered. The Supreme Court in Bureau of Investigation (CBI) v. Thommandru Hannah Vijayalakshmi (2021-10-08) (from a wider search, not independently verified) said that a preliminary enquiry is not mandatory before registering an FIR in corruption cases, and an FIR is not vitiated for want of one.
- Do not confuse Section 17 with Section 17A of the Act, which is a separate requirement: prior approval of the government or competent authority before any enquiry, inquiry or investigation into a public servant for a decision or recommendation made in discharge of official duties — a different stage and a different approval.
- Section 19 of the Act separately requires prior sanction before a court can take cognizance of an offence under Section 13, among others — again distinct from Section 17's investigation-stage requirement.
- What the material does not show: whether, in your specific case, the investigating officer's rank or the SP-level order under Section 17's second proviso was actually obtained; that is a factual question for the record.

What you can do now

1. Check the rank of the officer who investigated, and whether a Superintendent of Police's order exists for a Section 13(1)(b) case.
2. Ask for the case file or FIR details to see which magistrate's or SP's order, if any, was recorded.
3. Bring these specifics to a criminal lawyer handling Prevention of Corruption Act matters, since rank and sanction defects are assessed on the actual record.

Q2. “My relative is accused under section 19 of the POCSO Act for not reporting. What does section 19 require?

Ans -
Section 19 of the POCSO Act requires anyone -- including a child -- who knows that an offence under the Act has been committed, or who even suspects one is about to be, to inform the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police; failing that duty is what Section 21 of the POCSO Act punishes. This sounds serious for your relative, so let's look at exactly what the law asks for before worrying about the outcome.

What does Section 19 require?

Section 19(1) of the POCSO Act makes reporting mandatory, not optional, once a person has knowledge that an offence has occurred, or apprehends that one is likely to happen. It applies this duty "notwithstanding anything contained in" the earlier criminal procedure code, meaning it overrides any contrary general rule on who must report what. Section 19(2) sets out how the police must handle the report once made -- it must be given an entry number, recorded in writing, read back to the informant, and entered in a police register. Sections 19(3) and 19(4) add protections specifically for a child giving the report: it must be recorded in simple language the child understands, with a translator provided if needed. Section 19(5) requires the police, once satisfied a child needs care and protection, to arrange that within twenty-four hours. Section 19(6) requires the police to report the matter to the Child Welfare Committee and the Special Court within twenty-four hours. Section 19(7) protects anyone who gives information in good faith from civil or criminal liability for doing so.

The Supreme Court, in X v. Principal Secretary, Health and Family Welfare Department (from a wider search, not independently verified), described this same structure: that knowledge or apprehension of an offence triggers the duty, that failure to report is punishable under Section 21 of the POCSO Act, and that no fixed format is prescribed for the report.

What to keep in mind

- The duty under Section 19(1) of the POCSO Act turns on actual knowledge or apprehension -- not on mere suspicion or something a person merely heard of casually.
- Section 21(1) of the POCSO Act (punishment for failing to report) applies to ordinary persons; Section 21(2) sets a higher punishment specifically for someone in charge of a company or institution who fails to report about a subordinate.
- Section 21(3) of the POCSO Act exempts a child from this punishment entirely -- the reporting duty itself does not bind a child to criminal liability if unreported.
- Section 22 of the POCSO Act is a separate offence (false complaints) and should not be confused with Section 19/21.

What the material does not show: the specific facts of what your relative is said to have known or witnessed, which would decide whether the Section 19(1) threshold was even met.

What you can do right now

1. Ask for a copy of the FIR or complaint to see exactly what knowledge or apprehension is alleged.
2. Check whether your relative falls under Section 21(1) (general duty) or Section 21(2) (institutional in-charge), since the punishment differs.
3. Bring these documents to a lawyer handling POCSO matters to assess the knowledge/apprehension element specifically.

Q3. Can bail be refused under section 45 of the PMLA, and what are the conditions?
Ans-

Yes, bail can be refused under Section 45 of the PMLA, and it turns on two specific conditions the court must find satisfied before release, not on the usual bail test alone.

What does Section 45 require?

Section 45 of the PMLA begins with a clause that overrides the ordinary bail provisions of other criminal law when the two conflict. Before releasing a person accused of an offence under this Act, the court must be satisfied, first, that the public prosecutor has been given a chance to oppose the bail application, and second, if the prosecutor does oppose it, that there are reasonable grounds to believe the accused is not guilty and is not likely to commit any offence while on bail. The section ties into Section 19 of the PMLA, which covers the power to arrest a person believed to be guilty of a money-laundering offence, and into Section 4 of the PMLA, which sets out the punishment for money-laundering — so Section 45's bail bar applies to persons accused under this Act generally. A first proviso softens this for anyone under sixteen, a woman, someone sick or infirm, or someone accused (alone or with others) of laundering less than one crore rupees — the Special Court may grant them bail even without both conditions being met. A second proviso says the Special Court can only take up a complaint under Section 4 of the PMLA if it is filed in writing by the Director or an authorised government officer. An added clause clarifies that offences under the Act are always treated as cognizable and non-bailable, regardless of what general criminal procedure law would otherwise say.

The Supreme Court in Gautam Kundu v. Manoj Kumar (from a wider search, not independently verified) described these two conditions as mandatory before bail can be granted. In P. Chidambaram v. Directorate of Enforcement (from a wider search, not independently verified) and in The Union of India v. Kanhaiya Prasad (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Court repeated the same twin-condition framework, with the latter calling it well settled that both conditions must be complied with.

What else matters here?

- Arrest under Section 19 of the PMLA requires the arresting officer to record reasons in writing and inform the person of the grounds; the Supreme Court in Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India said this is essential so the arrested person can actually argue for bail under Section 45.
- A bail order can be contested at a higher court; Section 44 of the PMLA preserves the High Court's separate bail powers.
- What the material does not show: how courts assess "reasonable grounds to believe not guilty" on specific facts, or the appeal route from a Special Court's bail order.

What you can do now

1. Check whether a written complaint by the Director or an authorised officer has been filed, as the second proviso requires.
2. Ask whether the grounds of arrest under Section 19 of the PMLA were recorded and communicated in writing.
3. Bring any bail order or prosecutor's submissions to a lawyer handling PMLA matters to assess the twin-condition findings.

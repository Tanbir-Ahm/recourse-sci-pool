Q1: My wife filed a dowry harassment case under Section 498A and I was granted anticipatory bail. She says Nirmal Jeet Kaur v. State of M.P. (2004) means the High Court should not have granted it so easily. Is she right?


Your wife isn't on solid ground with that case — Nirmal Jeet Kaur v. State of M.P. (2004) is reported to have been overruled by Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2020) (from a wider search, not independently verified), so its holding can't be treated as settled law today. Whatever it said about anticipatory bail being narrow or hard to obtain is no longer the last word. That said, let's look at what actually governs this.

What the law says about pre-arrest bail

The offence your wife has alleged, cruelty under Section 85 of the BNS, is cognizable and non-bailable, carrying up to three years' imprisonment. Because it is non-bailable, Section 482 of the BNSS is the route for anticipatory bail: a person who believes he may be arrested can approach the High Court or Sessions Court for a direction that, if arrested, he be released on bail, subject to conditions like attending for interrogation and not tampering with evidence. This is discretionary, not automatic — the court weighs the facts before granting it.

Courts have also repeatedly flagged caution about arrest itself in cases like this. In Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar (2014), the Supreme Court directed that for offences punishable up to seven years, police must not arrest automatically but must first be satisfied that arrest is necessary, and record reasons. That reasoning was built specifically around Section 498-A cases and is the backdrop against which High Courts assess anticipatory bail pleas in matters like yours.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

Section 85 carries a maximum of three years, which is the category where courts are told to scrutinise the necessity of arrest closely.
Anticipatory bail having already been granted means a court has already weighed the facts and found grounds to protect you from custodial arrest.
Against you:

Anticipatory bail is conditional and discretionary — it can be reviewed or varied if the investigating agency shows new material.
If other provisions are later invoked, the calculus could change.
What I can't tell from here: what specific allegations or evidence the High Court considered, and whether the Public Prosecutor was heard before bail was granted.

What you can do right now

Keep a copy of the anticipatory bail order and its conditions handy, so you can show compliance if ever questioned.
Comply fully with any conditions attached — attending interrogation, not influencing witnesses — to avoid grounds for cancellation.
Ask your lawyer to explain, if your wife raises Nirmal Jeet Kaur formally, that it stands overruled and cannot be used to challenge the grant.


Q2. My brother was convicted of sedition by a TADA Designated Court and given life imprisonment. His lawyer is relying on Bilal Ahmed Kaloo v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997) for the appeal. How does that help him?

I need to flag something important before anything else: Bilal Ahmed Kaloo v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997) was overruled by State through Superintendent of Police, CBI/SIT v. Nalini and Others (1999). So if your brother's lawyer is building the appeal around this case as settled law, that foundation is no longer safe to rely on as-is.

What the case actually said

In Bilal Ahmed Kaloo, the accused had been tried before a Designated Court along with offences under the special law (TADA), but was acquitted of those special-law charges while being convicted of other offences. The point made there concerned confessional statements: once a person is acquitted of the special-law offences, a confession given to a police officer cannot be relied on for the remaining convictions, since that kind of confession is admissible only in a trial of a special-law offence. That reasoning was specific to how such confessions could be used — it was not a general ruling on sedition itself. And because the case was later overruled, its logic can't be cited to a court today as the current legal position.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

If your brother's conviction similarly rested partly on a confession made to a police officer, and he was acquitted of any special-law (TADA) charge, that specific evidentiary question may still be worth raising — but through a case that reflects the current position, not through Bilal Ahmed Kaloo itself.
If there are other grounds in the appeal (evidence, procedure, sentencing), those stand independently of this point.
Against you:

If the appeal's central plank is Bilal Ahmed Kaloo's reasoning, a court can simply note it was overruled and decline to apply it.
If your brother was convicted of the special-law offence itself (not just other offences after being acquitted of TADA charges), the fact pattern may not even match what Bilal Ahmed Kaloo addressed.
What I can't tell from here: whether your brother was acquitted of any TADA charge, whether a police confession was used against him, and what the other grounds of appeal are.

What you can do right now

Ask the lawyer directly whether the appeal relies on Bilal Ahmed Kaloo's holding or on some other point within it, and whether an alternative, current authority covers the same ground.
Bring up the overruling by Nalini explicitly in your conversation with the lawyer, so the appeal memo can be revised before filing or hearing.
Check whether your brother was in fact acquitted of any TADA offence and whether a police confession formed part of the conviction — this decides if the underlying point is even relevant.
Write down the full list of grounds the lawyer is raising, so you can ask each one is supported by law that still holds today.

Q3. The High Court stayed my corruption trial more than six months ago and has not extended the stay. My lawyer cites Asian Resurfacing of Road Agency Pvt. Ltd. v. CBI (2018) to say the stay has ended automatically. Is that right?

This question is about an area Recourse has not checked in depth (Recourse is built around police arrests, FIRs, criminal procedure and bail). What follows rests only on what we could find; please confirm it with a lawyer who handles this area before you rely on it.

Your lawyer's point needs a caveat before you rely on it: the automatic six-month stay-expiry rule from Asian Resurfacing of Road Agency Pvt. Ltd. v. CBI (2018) is reported to have been not approved by the Supreme Court in High Court Bar Association, Allahabad v. State of U.P. (2024) (from a wider search, not independently verified), specifically on the automatic-vacation-of-stay and day-to-day-hearing directions (not yet confirmed by our reviewer). So treating the stay as automatically over is not something you can safely assume.

What the law says now

The 2018 direction in Asian Resurfacing said that any stay of a civil or criminal trial would lapse after six months unless extended by a speaking order explaining why the stay was more important than finishing the trial. The idea was to stop stays from freezing trials indefinitely. But that specific direction is reported to have been not approved in the 2024 High Court Bar Association, Allahabad case (from a wider search, not independently verified), which leaves the rest of the 2018 judgment standing but calls out paragraphs 36 and 37 — the automatic vacation and day-to-day hearing directions — for correction. The 2024 passage itself simply notes this history; it does not spell out, in what is available here, what rule now replaces it.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

Six months without any extension order is a long gap, and the concern behind Asian Resurfacing — stays dragging on unexplained — is factually present here.
If the High Court never passed a speaking order justifying continuation, that absence may still matter to how the case is argued.
Against you:

The automatic-lapse rule your lawyer is citing is reported to be the very part not approved in 2024, so a court may not treat the stay as having ended on its own.
Without knowing what the 2024 bench put in its place, nobody can say the trial can simply resume without a fresh order.
What I can't tell from here: whether the High Court has since clarified its own stay order, and what replacement standard the 2024 decision set for stays that are already running.

What you can do right now

Ask your lawyer to check the full 2024 High Court Bar Association, Allahabad judgment for what it substitutes for the automatic-lapse rule.
Bring an application before the High Court itself asking it to confirm, vacate, or extend the stay, rather than assuming it has lapsed.
Check the trial court's own record for any date fixed for resumption, since that may show how the stay was last treated procedurally.

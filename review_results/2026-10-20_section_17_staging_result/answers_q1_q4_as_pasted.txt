Question 1 Can a disproportionate assets case under PC act be filed without section 17 approval?
Your situation
Yes, a disproportionate assets case can usually be registered and investigated without the approval required under Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, because that section deals with rank — not with a separate "approval to file the case" requirement.

What Section 17 actually requires

Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 says an officer below a certain rank cannot investigate an offence under this Act, or arrest without a warrant, unless a Metropolitan Magistrate or first-class Magistrate orders it. The ranks are: an Inspector of Police for the Delhi Special Police Establishment; an Assistant Commissioner of Police in specified metropolitan areas; and a Deputy Superintendent of Police (or equivalent) elsewhere. Its first proviso lets a State Government authorise an Inspector-rank officer to investigate or arrest without that magisterial order. Its second proviso adds that an offence under clause (b) of sub-section (1) of section 13 cannot be investigated without the order of an officer not below Superintendent of Police. The rule exists so that corruption investigations, which can be abused, are handled by officers of sufficient seniority — not to block registration of a case itself.

This is different from Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, which requires prior approval before any enquiry or investigation into a public servant's official decisions or recommendations — a separate safeguard for official-function cases, with its own provisos (no approval needed for a spot arrest for accepting an undue advantage; the competent authority must decide within three months, extendable by one month).

The Supreme Court, in Bureau of Investigation (CBI) and Anr v. Thommandru Hannah Vijayalakshmi (2021) (from a wider search, not independently verified), held that a preliminary enquiry is not mandatory before registering an FIR in corruption cases, including disproportionate-assets cases, where the information already discloses a cognizable offence.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

If the investigating officer held the rank Section 17 requires, or acted under the State Government's authorisation, the absence of separate "Section 17 approval" is not itself a defect.
Section 17 is about rank/authorisation to investigate, not a precondition to filing the FIR.
Against you:

If the officer was below the required rank and had no magisterial order or State authorisation, that is a real procedural gap worth raising.
If the case also involves scrutiny of official decisions, Section 17A approval (not Section 17) may separately be in question.
What I can't tell from here: the rank of the officer who registered/investigated the case, and whether any State authorisation existed.

What you can do now

Ask for the FIR and case diary to check the investigating officer's rank and any authorisation order.
Check whether the case also touches official decisions, which would bring Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 into play separately.
Bring these documents to a criminal lawyer to assess whether the rank/authorisation requirement under Section 17 was met.

Question 2 · you asked
What if  No section 17 order exists for this case of disproportionate assets even after the submission of the chargesheet and the source information report turned out to be false?
Your situation
Missing approval under Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 can matter even after the chargesheet is filed, but whether it undoes the case depends on rank of the investigating officer, what kind of defect it is, and whether it caused real prejudice.

What does Section 17 require?

Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 says that below a certain rank — Inspector for the Delhi Special Police Establishment, Assistant Commissioner of Police in the named metropolitan areas, or Deputy Superintendent of Police elsewhere — a police officer cannot investigate an offence under this Act, or arrest without warrant for it, unless a Metropolitan Magistrate or First Class Magistrate orders it. Its first proviso allows a State Government to authorise an Inspector-rank officer, by general or special order, to investigate or arrest without that Magistrate's order. Its second proviso says an offence falling under clause (b) of sub-section (1) of section 13 cannot be investigated without an order from an officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police. This rank requirement exists so that corruption investigations, which can seriously affect a public servant's career, are handled or supervised by sufficiently senior officers. The material here does not show a court ruling on the consequence of a missing Section 17 order, so I can't say how courts have treated that specific gap.

On the source information report turning out to be false: the Supreme Court in State of Karnataka v. T.N. Sudhakar Reddy (2025) (from a wider search, not independently verified) held, in the passage given, that such a report at the preliminary stage only needs to show a prima facie cognizable offence, not absolute truthfulness — a later finding that details in it were wrong does not itself undo that initial prima facie satisfaction.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

If no Magistrate's order existed and no State Government authorisation under the first proviso covered the officer's rank, that is a procedural gap worth raising.
If the source information turning out false also affects the disproportionate-assets figures in the chargesheet, that goes to the strength of the case, as in Chittaranjan Das v. State of Orissa (from a wider search, not independently verified), where the chargesheet's disproportionate-assets figure was far lower than the FIR's.
Against you:

If the investigating officer's own rank already met Section 17's threshold, no order was needed in the first place.
A report being later shown false does not retrospectively invalidate an FIR that disclosed a cognizable offence at the time, per Sudhakar Reddy.
What I can't tell from here: the investigating officer's rank, whether any State Government authorisation existed, and exactly what in the source report was false.

What you can do right now

Check the chargesheet and case diary for the investigating officer's rank and any Magistrate's order or State authorisation under Section 17.
Bring this specific gap, with dates, to your defence counsel to assess at the chargesheet stage.
Ask counsel whether the falsity found in the source report affects the disproportionate-assets computation itself, not just procedure.

Question 3 · you asked
The defect was in the FIR it said section 17 approval was given by the Chief Minister. Also the copy of section 17 order is non existent. It was never supplied to court
Your situation
This matters, but whether it helps you depends on what the FIR's wrong claim actually affected, not just that the claim was wrong.

What Section 17 of the PC Act requires

Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 says that an officer below a stated rank (Inspector in Delhi Special Police Establishment cases, Assistant Commissioner in certain metropolitan areas, or Deputy Superintendent elsewhere) cannot investigate a PC Act offence, or arrest without warrant for it, unless a Magistrate orders it. Its first proviso lets a State Government authorise an Inspector-rank officer by general or special order to do this without a Magistrate's order. Its second proviso says an offence under a specified clause of Section 13(1) cannot be investigated without an order from an officer not below Superintendent of Police. So the real question is: who gave the order authorising investigation, and at what rank — not who the FIR says approved something. Note that Section 17 governs who may investigate; it is different from Section 17A, which requires prior approval before investigating a public servant for an official decision — that is a separate provision with its own conditions.

The Supreme Court's approach in Bhajan Lal (as described in State of Madhya Pradesh v. Shri Ram Singh (from a wider search, not independently verified)) found that a mechanical, casual Section 17 authorisation did not meet the law's requirements. But that same case also noted that even without the Superintendent's authority, the investigating officer was in law authorised to investigate most PC Act offences. A wrongly described authorisation does not, by itself, mean no valid authorisation existed at all.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

If no valid Section 17 order exists at all, and the officer who investigated was below the required rank, this goes to the root of who could lawfully investigate.
A false statement in the FIR about who approved it raises a real question about the record's reliability.
Against you:

If an Inspector-rank officer was authorised by general State Government order under the first proviso, no fresh order was needed, regardless of the FIR's wording.
Courts weigh whether this objection was raised early, and whether the error caused real prejudice, not just whether paperwork was imperfect.
What I can't tell from here: the investigating officer's actual rank, whether any general or special State authorisation existed, and whether this was raised before the trial court.

What you can do right now

Ask the prosecution, through your counsel, to produce the actual Section 17 order or the general/special authorisation it relies on.
Raise the FIR's inconsistency about who gave approval as a specific objection before the trial court.
Check the investigating officer's rank at the time of registration against Section 17's requirement.
Bring this objection early, since courts consider delay in raising such objections.

Bottom line

The material does not contain the text of the Prevention of Corruption Act, so no section of that Act can be stated here as the governing provision. The wider-search passages indicate that courts have treated a preliminary inquiry or prior approval as not invariably mandatory before an FIR in a disproportionate-assets case, but whether any approval requirement applies, and its consequence if skipped, depends on the date of the alleged offence and facts not given.

Facts assumed

The question does not state the date of the alleged offence, whether an FIR has already been registered, or the forum where the matter is pending.

1. The governing statute is not before me

The person asks about "Section 17 approval" under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. That Act's text is not in the material supplied. Section 17 of the BNS, set out above, is the general exception for acts done under a bona fide mistaken belief of legal justification; it has no connection to approvals for investigating corruption offences and cannot be used as a stand-in for the Prevention of Corruption Act's own provisions. Any answer on the actual approval requirement, its scope, and its consequences must come from that Act and from case law interpreting it, not from the BNS.

2. Whether a prior-approval requirement applies at all turns on timing

In Nara Chandrababu Naidu v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2024) (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Court stated that the approval provision discussed there applies to offences after a 2018 amendment and not to offences existing before it. This suggests that whether any approval is required at all may depend on when the alleged acquisition of disproportionate assets occurred, a fact not given here.

3. A preliminary inquiry before an FIR is not treated as mandatory

In CBI v. Thommandru Hannah Vijayalakshmi (2021) (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Court held that an FIR is not vitiated merely because no preliminary enquiry was conducted, and credited an earlier decision (described in that passage as "Managipet") with holding that an officer may register an FIR in a disproportionate-assets case without a preliminary enquiry where prima facie information is available. State of Karnataka v. T.N. Sudhakar Reddy (2025) (from a wider search, not independently verified) similarly treated a preliminary inquiry as directed only at checking whether a cognizable offence is disclosed, not at verifying the truth of the information.

4. The FIR must still disclose prima facie ingredients of the offence

The State of Chhattisgarh v. Aman Kumar Singh (2023) (from a wider search, not independently verified) observed that an FIR in a disproportionate-assets case must, as a matter of necessity, prima facie disclose grounds to suspect "criminal misconduct" before investigation can proceed, since actual disproportion can only be confirmed once investigation is complete.

5. Absence of approval, where applicable, has been held not to automatically vitiate proceedings

The same passage in Nara Chandrababu Naidu (from a wider search, not independently verified) stated that even where an approval provision applies, its absence would neither vitiate the proceedings nor furnish a ground to quash the FIR. This passage does not address what remedy, if any, follows instead.

Grounds that can be urged

On these facts, it may be urged that no approval was obtained if the alleged conduct falls within the period to which the relevant approval provision applies, inviting the forum to consider the effect of that omission. It may also be urged, independently, that the FIR or charge-sheet lacks prima facie particulars of disproportion as required by the standard noted in Aman Kumar Singh (from a wider search, not independently verified). The State may in turn raise the position in Nara Chandrababu Naidu (from a wider search, not independently verified) that absence of approval does not vitiate proceedings.

Gaps and checks

The date of the alleged offence, the stage of proceedings, and the forum are not stated.

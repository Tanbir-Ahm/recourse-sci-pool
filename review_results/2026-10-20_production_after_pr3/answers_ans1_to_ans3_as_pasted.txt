Ans 1 - Section 35 of the BNSS sets out exactly when a police officer can arrest you without first getting a warrant or a magistrate's order, and the conditions depend on the offence and the circumstances.

What the law says

Section 35(1) of the BNSS lists several situations. The two most common: a cognizable offence committed in the officer's presence, under clause (a); or, under clause (b), a reasonable complaint, credible information, or reasonable suspicion of a cognizable offence punishable with up to seven years. For clause (b), the officer must also have "reason to believe" the person committed the offence, and separately be satisfied the arrest is necessary — for example to stop further offences, to properly investigate, to stop evidence being tampered with, or to ensure the person's presence in court. The officer must record these reasons in writing; if no arrest is made, reasons for not arresting must be recorded too.

This structure exists so that arrest is not automatic just because a complaint exists — the law builds in a check on police discretion. The Supreme Court in Satender Kumar Antil v. Central Bureau of Investigation (2026) (from a wider search, not independently verified) held that Section 35(1)(b)(i) of the BNSS and Section 35(1)(b)(ii) of the BNSS "must be read together," so satisfying the "reason to believe" condition is a sine qua non in every such arrest. In the same case the Court said the phrase "for proper investigation of the offence" in Section 35(1)(b)(ii) of the BNSS cannot be read as giving police unfettered discretion to arrest.

What to keep in mind

- For offences punishable up to seven years, Section 35(1)(b) of the BNSS requires both "reason to believe" the person committed it AND a specific necessity (listed in sub-clauses (a)-(e)) — not suspicion alone.
- Section 35(3) of the BNSS says that where arrest isn't required, the officer must instead issue a notice to appear; under Section 35(5) of the BNSS, a person who complies with the notice generally should not be arrested unless the officer records reasons for doing so anyway.
- Section 35(7) of the BNSS adds a safeguard: for offences punishable with less than three years, if the person is infirm or above sixty, arrest needs prior permission from an officer not below Deputy Superintendent of Police.
- For offences carrying more than seven years, or death, clause (c) applies with a lower threshold — credible information plus reason to believe is enough, without the extra necessity test.

What you can do now

1. Check which clause of Section 35(1) of the BNSS the offence in question falls under, since the punishment range changes what conditions apply.
2. Ask, if relevant, whether the arresting officer recorded reasons in writing, since Section 35(1)(b) of the BNSS requires this.
3. Bring the notice (if one was issued under Section 35(3) of the BNSS) to anyone advising you, since compliance with it affects whether arrest should follow.

Arresting your sister at night is not automatically illegal — it depends on whether exceptional circumstances existed and, if so, whether the proper written permission was obtained first. I can hear this is a frightening situation for your family.

What the law says about night arrests

Section 43(5) of the BNSS says that, save in exceptional circumstances, no woman is to be arrested after sunset and before sunrise. If such circumstances genuinely exist, a woman police officer must get prior written permission from a Judicial Magistrate of the first class before making the arrest. The rule exists to stop the cover of darkness being used to pressure or intimidate a woman without scrutiny.

How strictly this is enforced is not entirely settled. In Deepa v. S. Vijayalakshmi, the Madurai Bench of the Madras High Court treated this safeguard as directory rather than mandatory — meaning a breach does not, by itself, automatically void the arrest, though the police must still be able to justify the deviation. Separately, in Subhashree Das v. State of Orissa (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Supreme Court found an arrest was not unauthorised because it had happened before sunset, and it declined compensation on that basis — a different fact pattern, but it shows courts do check the actual clock-time, not just the general allegation of a "night arrest."

How this cuts for and against your sister

For her:
- If no exceptional circumstances existed and no Magistrate's written permission was obtained, this is a real safeguard she can point to.
- If the arresting officer was male, or if her submission to custody was not properly handled, that adds to the concern under Section 43(1).

Against her (factors a court may weigh the other way):
- If exceptional circumstances existed and written permission was taken, the night timing alone will not help her.
- Even if the night-arrest rule was breached, courts following Deepa may treat it as a factor, not an automatic ground to declare the arrest void.

What you can do right now

Courts have repeatedly held that an FIR in a disproportionate-assets case does not need a prior enquiry or approval before registration, so long as the source information discloses a prima facie cognizable offence. In State of Karnataka v. T.N. Sudhakar Reddy (2025) (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Court held that a source information report showing financial discrepancies and a clear picture of disproportionate assets was enough to justify investigation without needing to first verify its absolute truth. State of Andhra Pradesh (2024) (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Court said that the approval requirement applies only to conduct after a particular 2018 amendment, not to older conduct, and that its absence does not by itself make the proceedings invalid or justify quashing.

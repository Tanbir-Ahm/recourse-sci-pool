Ans 1 - Yes, a disproportionate assets case can generally be filed without prior approval under Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act, because that approval requirement applies only to a specific kind of conduct — not to every case under the Act.

What approval does Section 17A require?

Section 17A of the Prevention of Corruption Act requires prior approval before a police officer enquires into, inquires into, or investigates an offence where the alleged offence is relatable to a recommendation made or decision taken by a public servant in discharge of official functions or duties. It exists so that public servants aren't harassed for good-faith decisions taken in their official role. In Nara Chandrababu Naidu v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2024) (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Supreme Court said Section 17A applies only to offences after the 2018 amendment, and that even absence of this approval would not vitiate the proceedings or justify quashing the FIR.

A disproportionate-assets allegation is usually about possession of assets beyond known income, not about an official recommendation or decision — so Section 17A's condition may simply not be triggered on the facts at all. Separately, in State of Karnataka v. T.N. Sudhakar Reddy (2025) (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Court held that a source information report showing a prima facie picture of disproportionate assets was enough to justify investigative action, without needing to verify the information's absolute truth first.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

If the alleged conduct is simply holding disproportionate assets, Section 17A's condition about "recommendation or decision" may not even apply.
Even if 17A approval was missing, Nara Chandrababu Naidu says that alone would not vitiate the case.
Against you:

If part of the allegation ties specific assets to an official decision you took, that slice of the case could need Section 17A approval.
Separately, cognizance by the court may still need sanction under Section 19 of the Prevention of Corruption Act for a public servant, which is a different requirement from Section 17A.
What I can't tell from here: whether the specific allegations link any asset to an official decision or recommendation you made.

What you can do now

Check whether the FIR or chargesheet describes the alleged assets as connected to any specific official decision or recommendation.
Ask your lawyer to review whether Section 19 sanction (for cognizance) has been obtained, since that is separate from Section 17A.
Bring the FIR and any investigation order to a lawyer handling corruption-law matters for a point-by-point check against these sections.

Good question to clear up — Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act is a different safeguard from Section 17A, and it does restrict who can investigate a disproportionate-assets case.

What does Section 17 say?

Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act says that for offences under this Act, a police officer below a certain rank cannot investigate without the order of a Metropolitan Magistrate or first-class Magistrate, and cannot arrest without a warrant. The rank threshold depends on where the investigation happens: an Inspector for the Delhi Special Police Establishment, an Assistant Commissioner of Police in specified metropolitan areas, and a Deputy Superintendent of Police (or equivalent) elsewhere. The State Government can also authorise an Inspector-rank officer to investigate without that Magistrate's order, by a general or special order.

This exists so that corruption investigations, which can be used to harass public servants, are handled by a sufficiently senior or judicially-sanctioned officer rather than any constable. None of the material before me shows a court ruling specifically on what happens if a disproportionate-assets case is investigated in breach of Section 17 — so I can't tell you how courts have treated that particular defect, only what the section itself requires.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

If the investigating officer was below the required rank and had no Magistrate's order or State Government authorisation, that is a departure from Section 17's requirement.
If an arrest was made without a warrant by such an officer, that too falls outside what Section 17 permits.
Against you:

If the officer held the required rank (DSP or above, outside metropolitan areas), Section 17 is satisfied regardless of other objections.
If the State Government had issued a general order authorising Inspector-rank officers, that also satisfies Section 17.
What I can't tell from here: the rank of the officer who actually investigated, whether any Magistrate's order or State Government authorisation existed, and whether this defect was raised at the right stage.

What you can do now

Check the rank of the officer who conducted the investigation, and the location (metropolitan or not), since the required rank differs.
Ask for the Magistrate's order or the State Government's authorisation order, if any, that permitted the investigation.
Bring these specific documents to a criminal lawyer, since whether a Section 17 defect affects the case depends on when and how it is raised.

Yes — Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act does require this, specifically for the investigating rank, not a separate "registration" step.

What does Section 17 require?

Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act says that no police officer below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police (or equivalent) may investigate an offence under the Act without the order of a Metropolitan Magistrate or Magistrate of the first class. A further proviso says an offence under clause (b) of sub-section (1) of section 13 — which covers disproportionate assets — cannot be investigated without the order of a police officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police. So for a disproportionate assets case, SP-level authorisation to investigate is built into Section 17 itself.

This exists so that corruption investigations, which can be used to harass public servants, are screened at a senior enough level before they go forward. The Supreme Court in State of Karnataka (from a wider search, not independently verified) v. T.N. Sudhakar Reddy (2025) (from a wider search, not independently verified) rejected the idea that the SP must first direct registration of an FIR and only afterward issue the Section 17 investigation order — it held that Section 17's only requirement is that the SP authorise a competent subordinate officer to investigate, not that registration and authorisation happen in a fixed sequence.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

If the SP's authorisation was mechanical or showed no awareness of the allegations, State of Madhya Pradesh (from a wider search, not independently verified) v. Shri Ram Singh (2000) (from a wider search, not independently verified) noted that in an earlier case (Bhajan Lal) such an order was found non-compliant with Section 17.
If no SP-level order exists at all for the disproportionate assets allegation, that touches the specific proviso.
Against you:

If an SP did authorise investigation, even informally, T.N. Sudhakar Reddy (2025) treats that as sufficient without a strict FIR-first sequence.
Courts generally resist staying proceedings over such procedural points unless a failure of justice is shown.
What I can't tell from here: whether an SP-level order exists in this case, what it says, and whether it specifically addresses the disproportionate assets allegation.

What you can do now

Ask for a copy of the SP's authorisation order, if one exists, to see what it actually records.
Check whether the order was made before or in connection with the disproportionate assets investigation specifically.
Bring these documents to a criminal lawyer experienced in Prevention of Corruption Act matters to assess next steps.

The absence of a Section 17 order doesn't automatically wipe out a disproportionate assets case, but it does affect how the investigation can be challenged, and courts have treated this differently depending on the facts.

What the law says about this

Section 17 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 says an officer below the rank set out there cannot investigate a Section 13 offence (which covers disproportionate assets) without authorisation — and for the specific clause dealing with disproportionate assets, that authorisation must come from an officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police. This rank requirement exists so that sensitive corruption investigations aren't launched casually by junior officers.

In State of Madhya Pradesh (from a wider search, not independently verified) v. Shri Ram Singh (2000) (from a wider search, not independently verified), the Supreme Court noted that in an earlier case (Bhajan Lal), the SP had authorised investigation "mechanically and in a very casual manner," without complying with Section 17 — but the Court also observed that even without the SP's authority, the investigating officer was in law authorised to investigate offences under Section 13 except for the clause needing SP-level approval. In State of Karnataka v. T.N. Sudhakar Reddy (2025) (from a wider search, not independently verified), the passage describes a High Court's four-step procedure under which the SP only becomes competent to authorise investigation after the FIR is registered — meaning the order's validity can turn on the sequence followed, not just its existence.

How this cuts for and against you

For you:

If the offence falls under the specific disproportionate-assets clause, the second proviso to Section 17 requires SP-level authorisation, and its complete absence is a real procedural gap.
A missing or defective order can be raised before the trial court or in a quash petition, depending on the stage.
Against you:

Courts have, in some instances, held that absence of proper authorisation doesn't void the whole investigation outright, if other authority to investigate existed.
Whether the order was required to exist before or after FIR registration may itself be disputed, per the Sudhakar Reddy passage.
What I can't tell from here: which specific clause of Section 13 is invoked, the stage of your case (FIR, charge sheet, trial), and whether any order exists in a different form.

What you can do right now

Ask for a certified copy of the case diary or investigation record to check if any Section 17 order was passed, in whatever form.
Raise the absence of authorisation as a specific ground before the trial court, citing the rank requirement.
Bring this point to a lawyer handling corruption-law matters, to assess whether it supports a quash petition at this stage.
